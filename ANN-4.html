The full question

Retake Exam · Question 1 · 3 → 3 → 3 network · tanh + softmax + CCE

A feed-forward network has 3 inputs, 3 Tanh hidden neurons and 3 Softmax output neurons. Given:

\[ x=\begin{bmatrix} 1.0 \\ 0.5 \\ -0.5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad y=\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ W^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix} 0.4 & 0.0 & 0.4 \\ 0.4 & -0.5 & 0.2 \\ 0.2 & 0.5 & -0.2 \end{bmatrix} \qquad b^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0.1 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ W^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix} 0.3 & 0.1 & 0.5 \\ 0.2 & 0.4 & -0.1 \\ -0.3 & 0.2 & 0.6 \end{bmatrix} \qquad b^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0.1 \\ -0.1 \end{bmatrix} \]

The learning rate is given as \(\eta=0.3\), and the pre-activations use \((W^{(1)})^{T}\) and \((W^{(2)})^{T}\).

Answer the following:

  • (a)Compute \(z^{(1)}\) and \(h=\tanh(z^{(1)})\).
  • (b)Compute \(z^{(2)}\) and the Softmax probabilities \(\hat y\).
  • (c)Compute the categorical cross-entropy loss.
  • (d)Compute \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(2)}}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial b^{(2)}}\).
  • (e)State the Universal Approximation Theorem and explain what it guarantees and what it does not.
Note that \(\eta=0.3\) is given but never used — the question stops at the gradients and never asks for a weight update. Do not invent an update step; compute what is asked. (If you want the update anyway as practice, it is \(\theta-0.3\nabla\theta\).)

The shortcut the paper supplies for this pairing:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z^{(2)}_k}=\hat y_k-y_k } \]

The network at a glance

Before solving: shapes and wiring

Both weight matrices are \(3\times3\) here, so the transposes are easy to lose track of. Keep the rule: stored input-by-output, transposed in the forward pass.

    x (3)               h (3)                y^ (3)
   ┌──────┐   W(1)ᵀ   ┌──────┐   W(2)ᵀ     ┌──────────┐
   │  1.0 │ ────────► │ tanh │ ──────────► │          │ ─► y^₁ = 0.238
   │  0.5 │  + b(1)   │ tanh │   + b(2)    │ Softmax  │ ─► y^₂ = 0.295
   │ -0.5 │           │ tanh │             │          │ ─► y^₃ = 0.467  ← correct class
   └──────┘           └──────┘             └──────────┘
        3×3                    3×3                 │
                                          y = [0,0,1]ᵀ ─► CCE ─► L

Same forward pass as ANN-3, one layer wider. The genuinely new part is (d): the backward step through the output layer.

(a) Hidden layer — pre-activations and Tanh

\[ \boxed{ z^{(1)}=(W^{(1)})^{T}x+b^{(1)} } \qquad (W^{(1)})^{T}=\begin{bmatrix} 0.4 & 0.4 & 0.2 \\ 0 & -0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.4 & 0.2 & -0.2 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ z^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix} 0.4 & 0.4 & 0.2 \\ 0 & -0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.4 & 0.2 & -0.2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0.5 \\ -0.5 \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0.1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Hidden neuron 1

\[ z^{(1)}_1 = 0.4(1)+0.4(0.5)+0.2(-0.5)+0 = 0.4+0.2-0.1 \qquad \boxed{ z^{(1)}_1 = 0.50 } \]

Hidden neuron 2

\[ z^{(1)}_2 = 0(1)+(-0.5)(0.5)+0.5(-0.5)+0 = -0.25-0.25 \qquad \boxed{ z^{(1)}_2 = -0.50 } \]

Hidden neuron 3

\[ z^{(1)}_3 = 0.4(1)+0.2(0.5)+(-0.2)(-0.5)+0.1 = 0.4+0.1+0.1+0.1 \qquad \boxed{ z^{(1)}_3 = 0.70 } \]
\[ \boxed{ z^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix} 0.50 \\ -0.50 \\ 0.70 \end{bmatrix} } \]

Apply Tanh

\[ \tanh(0.5)\approx 0.462 \qquad \tanh(-0.5)\approx -0.462 \qquad \tanh(0.7)\approx 0.604 \]
\[ \boxed{ h \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.462 \\ -0.462 \\ 0.604 \end{bmatrix} } \]

Note the symmetry — tanh is an odd function:

\[ \tanh(-z)=-\tanh(z) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{ h_2=-h_1 } \]

You only need to evaluate \(\tanh(0.5)\) once. This also means a ReLU network could not produce this pattern — ReLU would have set \(h_2=0\) and killed that unit's gradient entirely. Tanh keeps the negative signal alive.

(b) Output layer — logits and Softmax

\[ \boxed{ z^{(2)}=(W^{(2)})^{T}h+b^{(2)} } \qquad (W^{(2)})^{T}=\begin{bmatrix} 0.3 & 0.2 & -0.3 \\ 0.1 & 0.4 & 0.2 \\ 0.5 & -0.1 & 0.6 \end{bmatrix} \]

Output 1

\[ z^{(2)}_1 = 0.3(0.462)+0.2(-0.462)-0.3(0.604)+0 \] \[ = 0.1386-0.0924-0.1812 \qquad \boxed{ z^{(2)}_1 \approx -0.135 } \]

The model answer prints \(-0.134\) here; the arithmetic above gives \(-0.135\), and so does carrying full precision (\(-0.1351\)). The difference is a rounding artefact and it changes nothing downstream — the probabilities agree to 3 decimal places either way. Use \(-0.135\) and show your working.

Output 2

\[ z^{(2)}_2 = 0.1(0.462)+0.4(-0.462)+0.2(0.604)+0.1 \] \[ = 0.0462-0.1848+0.1208+0.1 \qquad \boxed{ z^{(2)}_2 \approx 0.082 } \]

Output 3

\[ z^{(2)}_3 = 0.5(0.462)-0.1(-0.462)+0.6(0.604)-0.1 \] \[ = 0.231+0.0462+0.3624-0.1 \qquad \boxed{ z^{(2)}_3 \approx 0.539 } \]
\[ \boxed{ z^{(2)} \approx \begin{bmatrix} -0.135 \\ 0.082 \\ 0.539 \end{bmatrix} } \]

Apply Softmax

\[ \hat y_k=\frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} \]
\[ e^{-0.135}\approx 0.874 \qquad e^{0.082}\approx 1.085 \qquad e^{0.539}\approx 1.714 \] \[ S = 0.874+1.085+1.714 \qquad \boxed{ S \approx 3.674 } \]
\[ \hat y_1=\frac{0.874}{3.674}\approx 0.238 \qquad \hat y_2=\frac{1.085}{3.674}\approx 0.295 \qquad \hat y_3=\frac{1.714}{3.674}\approx 0.467 \]
\[ \boxed{ \hat y \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.238 \\ 0.295 \\ 0.467 \end{bmatrix} }, \qquad 0.238+0.295+0.467=1 \;\checkmark \]

The network already ranks class 3 highest, but only at \(46.7\%\) — confident enough to be right, not confident enough to have a small loss.

(c) Categorical cross-entropy loss

\[ L=-\sum_{k=1}^{3} y_k\ln\hat y_k, \qquad y=\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Only \(y_3=1\) survives, so the sum collapses to one term:

\[ L=-\ln(\hat y_3)=-\ln(0.467) \]
\[ \boxed{ L \approx 0.762 } \]

Same one-hot rule as ANN-3: \(L=-\ln(\text{probability of the correct class})\). For reference, a uniform guess over 3 classes would give \(-\ln(1/3)=1.099\), so \(0.762\) means the network is doing better than chance here — but not by a lot.

(d) Output-layer gradients

For Softmax + categorical cross-entropy, the two derivatives collapse into one clean expression — this is the pairing where the shortcut is genuinely valid, and the paper states it outright:

\[ \boxed{ \delta^{(2)}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial z^{(2)}}=\hat y-y } \]
\[ \delta^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix} 0.238 \\ 0.295 \\ 0.467 \end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \boxed{ \delta^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.238 \\ 0.295 \\ -0.533 \end{bmatrix} } \]

Read the signs — they are the whole story:

  • The two wrong classes have positive deltas: their probabilities need to come down.
  • The correct class has a negative delta, because its predicted \(0.467\) is below its target of \(1\): its probability needs to go up.

Gradient of \(W^{(2)}\)

Since \(z^{(2)}=(W^{(2)})^{T}h+b^{(2)}\), the gradient is the outer product:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(2)}}=h\,(\delta^{(2)})^{T} } \qquad \underbrace{h}_{3\times1}\;\underbrace{(\delta^{(2)})^{T}}_{1\times3} \;\Rightarrow\; \underbrace{\nabla W^{(2)}}_{3\times3} \] \[ \begin{bmatrix} 0.462 \\ -0.462 \\ 0.604 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0.238 & 0.295 & -0.533 \end{bmatrix} \]

Row 1 (from \(h_1=0.462\))

\[ 0.462(0.238)\approx 0.110 \qquad 0.462(0.295)\approx 0.136 \qquad 0.462(-0.533)\approx -0.246 \]

Row 2 (from \(h_2=-0.462\))

\[ -0.462(0.238)\approx -0.110 \qquad -0.462(0.295)\approx -0.136 \qquad -0.462(-0.533)\approx 0.246 \]

Because \(h_2=-h_1\), row 2 is just \(-1\times\) row 1. No new multiplications needed — copy row 1 and flip every sign.

Row 3 (from \(h_3=0.604\))

\[ 0.604(0.238)\approx 0.144 \qquad 0.604(0.295)\approx 0.178 \qquad 0.604(-0.533)\approx -0.322 \]
\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(2)}} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.110 & 0.136 & -0.246 \\ -0.110 & -0.136 & 0.246 \\ 0.144 & 0.178 & -0.322 \end{bmatrix} } \]

Gradient of \(b^{(2)}\)

The bias has \(\partial z^{(2)}_k/\partial b^{(2)}_k=1\), so the gradient is the delta itself:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial L}{\partial b^{(2)}} = \delta^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.238 \\ 0.295 \\ -0.533 \end{bmatrix} } \]

Why the weight gradient is an outer product

Worth understanding once, because it repeats in every layer of every network you will ever differentiate.

Each output-layer weight \(w^{(2)}_{ij}\) connects exactly one hidden activation to one logit: \(h_i\rightarrow z^{(2)}_j\). It appears in the forward pass in exactly one product, \(w^{(2)}_{ij}h_i\), so:

\[ \frac{\partial z^{(2)}_j}{\partial w^{(2)}_{ij}}=h_i \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w^{(2)}_{ij}} = h_i\,\delta_j } \]

Every hidden activation multiplied by every output error — and "every row entry times every column entry" is the outer product \(h\delta^{T}\).

Spot-check one entry

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w^{(2)}_{13}} = h_1\delta_3 = 0.462(-0.533) \approx -0.246 \;\checkmark \]

It sits in row 1, column 3 of the matrix above. If you are ever unsure whether a gradient should be \(h\delta^{T}\) or \(\delta h^{T}\), compute one entry this way and see which orientation puts it in the right place — the answer must have the same shape as \(W^{(2)}\).

Three free sanity checks

All three cost seconds and catch most arithmetic slips.

1. Softmax outputs sum to 1

\[ 0.238+0.295+0.467 = 1.000 \;\checkmark \]

2. The deltas sum to zero

Always true for Softmax + CCE with a one-hot target, since \(\sum_k\hat y_k=1\) and \(\sum_k y_k=1\):

\[ \sum_k \delta^{(2)}_k = \sum_k\hat y_k-\sum_k y_k = 1-1 = 0 \] \[ 0.238+0.295-0.533 = 0 \;\checkmark \]

3. Every row of \(\nabla W^{(2)}\) sums to zero

This follows immediately from check 2, because row \(i\) is \(h_i\) times the delta vector:

\[ \sum_j h_i\delta_j = h_i\sum_j\delta_j = h_i\cdot 0 = 0 \] \[ \text{Row 1: } 0.110+0.136-0.246 = 0 \;\checkmark \] \[ \text{Row 2: } -0.110-0.136+0.246 = 0 \;\checkmark \] \[ \text{Row 3: } 0.144+0.178-0.322 = 0 \;\checkmark \]

If a row does not sum to zero, you have a slip in that row — and you know it is in the row, not in \(\delta\).

(e) Universal Approximation Theorem

Statement

A feed-forward neural network with at least one hidden layer containing sufficiently many neurons, and a non-constant, bounded, continuous activation function such as Sigmoid or Tanh, can approximate any continuous function on a compact subset of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) to arbitrary accuracy.

What it guarantees

For any target accuracy \(\epsilon>0\) there exists a network \(F\) large enough that

\[ |F(x)-f(x)|<\epsilon \quad\text{for all } x \text{ in the compact domain.} \]
\[ \boxed{ \text{A suitable network \textbf{exists} that approximates the target function arbitrarily well.} } \]

What it does not guarantee

It does not sayWhy that matters
How many neurons are requiredThe bound can be astronomically large; the theorem is silent on size.
That training will find those weightsIt is an existence result, not a statement about gradient descent. The weights exist; optimisation may never reach them.
That the network will generaliseApproximation is on the training domain. Nothing is promised about unseen data.
That it holds for any activationIn the form stated here it needs a bounded, continuous, non-constant activation — a discontinuous step function is excluded.

The one-line summary to write if you are short on time: the theorem is about existence, not about learnability, size, or generalisation.

Final answer sheet

Forward pass

\[ \boxed{ z^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix} 0.50 \\ -0.50 \\ 0.70 \end{bmatrix} } \qquad \boxed{ h \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.462 \\ -0.462 \\ 0.604 \end{bmatrix} } \] \[ \boxed{ z^{(2)} \approx \begin{bmatrix} -0.135 \\ 0.082 \\ 0.539 \end{bmatrix} } \qquad \boxed{ \hat y \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.238 \\ 0.295 \\ 0.467 \end{bmatrix} } \qquad \boxed{ L \approx 0.762 } \]

Backward pass

\[ \boxed{ \delta^{(2)}=\hat y-y=\begin{bmatrix} 0.238 \\ 0.295 \\ -0.533 \end{bmatrix} } \] \[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(2)}} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.110 & 0.136 & -0.246 \\ -0.110 & -0.136 & 0.246 \\ 0.144 & 0.178 & -0.322 \end{bmatrix} } \qquad \boxed{ \frac{\partial L}{\partial b^{(2)}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.238 \\ 0.295 \\ -0.533 \end{bmatrix} } \]

The pattern, now complete

\[ \boxed{ \text{Softmax + CCE} \;\Rightarrow\; \delta=\hat y-y \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla W=h\,\delta^{T}, \quad \nabla b=\delta } \]
