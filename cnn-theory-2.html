1. LeNet-5 — Why is LeNet considered a foundational CNN architecture?

foundational hierarchy
QuestionExplain the basic architecture of LeNet-5 and why it was important in the development of convolutional neural networks.

LeNet-5 is an early CNN designed mainly for handwritten digit recognition. A \(32\times32\times1\) image passes through a \(5\times5\) convolution to produce \(28\times28\times6\), followed by \(2\times2\) average pooling. A second \(5\times5\) convolution produces \(10\times10\times16\), followed by another pooling layer, and finally fully connected layers of 120 and 84 neurons before classification.

  32×32×1 ──5×5 conv──► 28×28×6 ──2×2 pool──► 14×14×6
          ──5×5 conv──► 10×10×16 ──2×2 pool──►  5×5×16
          ──flatten──► FC 120 ──► FC 84 ──► classify

Its important idea is the repeated pattern of convolution followed by spatial downsampling. Convolution extracts local patterns such as edges and shapes, while pooling progressively reduces spatial resolution and makes the representation more compact.

Thus LeNet established the basic CNN idea that modern networks still follow: early layers learn local features and deeper layers combine them into increasingly abstract representations.

Check the spatial arithmetic with \(n_{out}=\lfloor(n+2p-k)/s\rfloor+1\): valid \(5\times5\) on 32 gives \(32-5+1=28\); \(2\times2\) pooling with \(s=2\) halves it to 14; then \(14-5+1=10\), and pooling gives 5. Every number in the chain follows from the one formula.

2. AlexNet — How did AlexNet extend the ideas of LeNet?

scale and capacity
QuestionCompare the design philosophy of AlexNet with LeNet-5. Why was AlexNet suitable for much more complex image recognition?

AlexNet follows the same broad CNN principle as LeNet but operates on much larger RGB images and uses a substantially larger and deeper feature hierarchy. It takes a \(227\times227\times3\) image, begins with a large \(11\times11\) convolution with stride 4, followed by max pooling, then several convolutional layers with hundreds of channels before reaching fully connected layers of 4096 neurons and a 1000-class Softmax output.

The key conceptual change is scale and capacity. LeNet handled relatively simple grayscale digits, whereas AlexNet had to recognise complex natural objects with variation in colour, background, texture and viewpoint. It therefore uses many more channels and deeper feature extraction.

As depth increases, early layers can detect simple features while later layers represent more complicated object parts and semantic patterns.

LeNet-5AlexNet
Input\(32\times32\times1\)\(227\times227\times3\)
First kernel\(5\times5,\ s=1\)\(11\times11,\ s=4\)
Channel scale6 → 1696 → hundreds
FC layers120, 844096, 4096
Classes10 digits1000 objects

Note the stride-4 first layer: at \(227\times227\) a stride-1 convolution would be ruinously expensive, so AlexNet downsamples aggressively and immediately — \((227-11)/4+1=55\). This is the cost argument from the MACs page showing up as an architectural decision.

3. VGG — Why does VGG repeatedly use \(3\times3\) convolutions?

small kernels, stacked
QuestionVGG-16 mainly uses repeated \(3\times3\) convolution layers instead of large convolution kernels. What is the intuition behind this design?

VGG uses a very uniform design: most convolution layers use \(3\times3\) filters with stride 1 and same padding, while \(2\times2\) max pooling reduces spatial resolution. The network is built from repeated blocks — two \(3\times3\) convolutions at 64 and 128 channels, then blocks of three convolutions at 256 and 512 channels.

Stacking small kernels lets the network gradually build larger receptive fields while inserting nonlinear transformations between layers. Two consecutive \(3\times3\) convolutions give a \(5\times5\) receptive field, and three give \(7\times7\).

Thus VGG obtains large-context features using a simple, repeated architecture rather than relying on large kernels everywhere.

DesignReceptive fieldParameters (per channel pair)Non-linearities
One \(7\times7\) conv\(7\times7\)491
Three \(3\times3\) convs\(7\times7\)\(3\times9=27\)3

Same view of the image, 45% fewer parameters, and three ReLUs instead of one. That is the whole argument in one row. The receptive-field claim is derived in full on the receptive field page — the key point being that overlapping windows make stacked \(3\times3\)s add \(2\) each time rather than multiply.

4. VGG — Why do spatial dimensions decrease while channels increase?

resolution traded for semantics
QuestionIn VGG-16, why does the feature-map size decrease from \(224\times224\) toward \(7\times7\), while the number of channels increases from 64 to 512?

The spatial dimensions are reduced by max pooling:

\[ 224\times224 \rightarrow 112\times112 \rightarrow 56\times56 \rightarrow 28\times28 \rightarrow 14\times14 \rightarrow 7\times7 \]

while the channel depth grows \(64 \rightarrow 128 \rightarrow 256 \rightarrow 512\).

The intuition is that early layers need high spatial resolution to detect local patterns such as edges and textures. Deeper layers no longer need exact pixel-level location to the same extent; instead they need a richer collection of semantic features.

Therefore spatial detail is gradually compressed while the number of learned feature types increases. Each halving of resolution divides the number of spatial positions by 4, so doubling the channels roughly keeps the per-layer cost balanced instead of letting it explode at the front or collapse at the back.

There is also a receptive-field consequence: each pooling stage doubles the jump \(j\), so the same \(3\times3\) kernel reaches further into the original image at every successive block. Downsampling buys both cheaper computation and faster context growth.

5. ResNet — What problem does a residual connection solve?

★ highest-value conceptual question
QuestionWhat is a residual block in ResNet, and why does it make very deep neural networks easier to train?

A normal block tries to directly learn a desired mapping \(H(x)\). A residual block instead learns a residual function \(F(x)\) and adds the original input through a shortcut connection:

\[ \boxed{ y=F(x)+x } \]
          ┌──────────── identity shortcut ────────────┐
          │                                           ▼
   x ─────┴──► [ conv → ReLU → conv ] ──► F(x) ──►   (+)  ──► y = F(x) + x

Information can travel through the normal convolutional path as well as directly through the identity shortcut.

The intuitive advantage is that if the extra layers are not useful, the network does not need to learn a complicated transformation — it can make \(F(x)\) close to zero, giving approximately \(y=x\). The shortcut also gives gradients a more direct route during backpropagation.

Therefore adding many layers to a ResNet is much less likely to cause the optimisation degradation seen in a similarly deep plain network.

6. ResNet — Why is learning a residual often easier than learning the full mapping?

the residual intuition
QuestionExplain intuitively why ResNet learns \(F(x)=H(x)-x\) instead of directly learning \(H(x)\).

Suppose the ideal transformation of some layers should be nearly the identity, \(H(x)\approx x\). A plain network must learn the complete identity mapping through several nonlinear layers — which is surprisingly awkward, since stacked convolutions and ReLUs are not naturally identity-shaped.

In a residual network, since \(H(x)=F(x)+x\), the network only has to learn

\[ F(x)=H(x)-x\approx 0 \]

and driving a set of weights toward zero is easy. Learning a small correction to an already useful representation is therefore easier than reconstructing the whole representation from scratch.

This gives a useful interpretation of residual blocks. Each block asks

\[ \boxed{ \text{"What modification should I make to the current features?"} } \]

rather than "What completely new features should replace them?". As a result, deep ResNets progressively refine representations while retaining information from earlier layers.

7. ResNet — What is the difference between a plain block and a residual block?

plain vs residual
QuestionExplain how a residual block differs from an ordinary sequence of convolutional layers.

In a plain network, the input passes through several transformations and only the transformed output is passed forward. In a residual block, the original input \(x\) is also carried through a shortcut path and added to the transformed features:

\[ \boxed{ y=F(x)+x } \]

Thus the block learns a residual correction \(F(x)\) rather than replacing the entire input representation.

This means useful information can travel directly across the block. If the newly added layers are not useful, the learned residual can approach zero and the block approximately becomes an identity mapping. That makes increasing network depth much safer than in an ordinary plain CNN.

8. ResNet — Why can adding layers hurt a plain network but help a ResNet?

the degradation problem
QuestionA deeper network should theoretically be at least as powerful as a shallower one. Why can a very deep plain CNN have higher training error, while ResNet avoids this problem?

Adding layers increases representational capacity, but it also makes the optimisation problem more difficult. In a plain network, every additional layer has to learn an appropriate transformation, and very deep compositions may become hard to optimise.

Note carefully what is being claimed: the deeper plain network has higher training error, not just higher test error. So this is not overfitting — a deeper network could in principle copy the shallower one and add identity layers, and match it exactly. The problem is that gradient descent does not find that solution.

ResNet provides shortcut paths through which information and gradients can flow directly. If extra layers are unnecessary, they can learn an approximately zero residual instead of having to reproduce the identity transformation themselves. Therefore ResNet makes additional depth easier to exploit during training.

\[ \boxed{ \text{The problem was never capacity. It was optimisability.} } \]

9. Inception — Why does an Inception module use several filter sizes in parallel?

★ highest-value conceptual question
QuestionExplain the main idea of an Inception module. Why are \(1\times1\), \(3\times3\), \(5\times5\) convolution and pooling performed in parallel?

The Inception module allows the network to process the same input using several operations simultaneously. Branches containing \(1\times1\), \(3\times3\), \(5\times5\) convolutions and max pooling all receive the same input, and their outputs are concatenated along the channel dimension.

                ┌──► 1×1 conv ─────────┐
                │                       │
   input ───────┼──► 3×3 conv ─────────┼──► channel concat ──► output
                │                       │
                ├──► 5×5 conv ─────────┤
                │                       │
                └──► 3×3 max pool ─────┘

The intuition is that different kernel sizes capture information at different spatial scales. A \(1\times1\) convolution focuses on channel combinations at a single location, while \(3\times3\) and \(5\times5\) filters observe progressively larger neighbourhoods.

Instead of forcing the designer to choose one kernel size, Inception lets the network retain features produced at several scales and combine them into one richer representation. The choice becomes something the network learns to weight, rather than a hyperparameter someone has to guess.

10. Inception — Why are \(1\times1\) convolutions used before expensive \(3\times3\) or \(5\times5\) convolutions?

★ highest-value conceptual question
QuestionExplain how a \(1\times1\) convolution reduces the computational cost of an Inception module.

A large convolution becomes expensive when the input has many channels. Take a \(28\times28\times192\) activation processed by a \(5\times5\) convolution. Rather than applying the expensive \(5\times5\) convolution directly across all 192 channels, a \(1\times1\) convolution first reduces the channel dimension — for example from 192 to 16 — and the \(5\times5\) convolution then operates on this smaller representation.

Therefore the \(1\times1\) layer acts as a bottleneck, or dimensionality-reduction layer. It preserves spatial height and width while reducing the number of channels, dramatically decreasing the multiplications required by the following larger convolution.

Thus Inception can obtain multi-scale features without making computational cost unreasonable.

Putting numbers on it

Taking the usual 32 output channels for the \(5\times5\) branch, and counting MACs as on the computational cost page:

PathComputationMACs

Bottleneck \(1\times1\), \(192\rightarrow16\)\(28^2\times16\times192\)2,408,448
Then \(5\times5\), \(16\rightarrow32\)\(28^2\times32\times(5^2\times16)\)10,035,200
Bottleneck total—12,443,648
\[ \frac{120{,}422{,}400}{12{,}443{,}648} \approx \boxed{9.7\times\text{ cheaper}} \qquad \boxed{\approx 90\%\text{ saving}} \]

Note where the saving comes from: the \(5\times5\) kernel now spans 16 channels instead of 192, so the \(K^2C_{in}\) term drops by a factor of 12. The cheap \(1\times1\) layer costs a little, and buys a lot.

11. Inception — What does "channel concatenation" mean?

concat, not add
QuestionIn an Inception module, \(1\times1\), \(3\times3\), \(5\times5\) convolution and pooling branches operate in parallel. How are their outputs combined?

Each branch receives the same input activation and produces its own set of feature maps. These outputs are not added together. Instead they are concatenated along the channel dimension, at the operation labelled "Channel Concat".

For example, if four branches produce 64, 128, 32 and 32 output channels while preserving the same height and width, the final output depth becomes

\[ 64+128+32+32 = \boxed{256} \]

Thus the final representation simultaneously contains features extracted at several spatial scales.

Contrast this with ResNet, which adds ( \(F(x)+x\) ) and so keeps the channel count fixed while merging the two signals. Concatenation keeps the branches separable as distinct channels; addition blends them. This is the same distinction as ResNet vs DenseNet in cnn-1.

Because the outputs are stacked, all branches must produce the same spatial size — which is why the convolution branches use same padding.

12. Inception — Why is pooling also included as a branch?

keep several options
QuestionWhy does an Inception module include a max-pooling branch in addition to convolution branches?

Different operations capture different kinds of information. The \(1\times1\), \(3\times3\) and \(5\times5\) branches learn features at different spatial scales, while the pooling branch provides a different form of local aggregation — it summarises a neighbourhood by its strongest response rather than by a learned weighted sum.

The design therefore avoids committing the entire layer to a single operation. Instead, the network can preserve convolution-derived features together with pooled features.

In intuitive terms, an Inception module asks

\[ \boxed{ \text{"Which type of local processing is most useful here?"} } \]

and keeps the outputs of several possibilities rather than choosing only one. The selection is deferred to training, which learns how much to rely on each branch.

13. MobileNet — Why is MobileNet computationally efficient?

★ highest-value conceptual question
QuestionWhat is the main idea behind MobileNet, and how does depthwise-separable convolution reduce computational cost?

MobileNet is designed for situations where deployment cost matters, particularly mobile and embedded vision. Its key idea is replacing ordinary convolution with depthwise-separable convolution. A normal convolution performs spatial filtering and channel mixing together; a depthwise-separable convolution splits them into a depthwise convolution followed by a \(1\times1\) pointwise convolution.

The depthwise stage applies one spatial filter to each input channel independently, so its cost is approximately

\[ H_oW_oK^{2}C_{in} \]

The pointwise stage then mixes channels with cost

\[ H_oW_oC_{in}C_{out} \]

while a standard convolution would cost

\[ H_oW_oK^{2}C_{in}C_{out} \]

By separating spatial filtering from channel mixing, MobileNet removes much of the redundant computation and therefore achieves a much lighter model suitable for resource-limited devices.

\[ \frac{MAC_{\text{DS}}}{MAC_{\text{standard}}} = \frac{1}{C_{out}}+\frac{1}{K^{2}} \]

Note the structure of the saving: the standard form multiplies \(K^2\) by \(C_{out}\), the separable form adds them. For a \(3\times3\) kernel that ratio is dominated by \(1/9\), so the saving is roughly \(8\)–\(9\times\) essentially regardless of the layer's size. Derived in full on the computational cost page.

14. MobileNet — Why is pointwise convolution still necessary after depthwise convolution?

spatial vs channel
QuestionIf depthwise convolution already applies a spatial filter to every channel, why does MobileNet need a \(1\times1\) pointwise convolution afterward?

Depthwise convolution processes every input channel independently. It can therefore detect spatial patterns inside each channel, but it does not mix information between different channels. Without a second stage, the channels would never interact and the layer could not even change the channel count.

MobileNet solves this by following the depthwise stage with a \(1\times1\) pointwise convolution. The pointwise convolution looks across all input channels at each spatial location, allowing information from different channels to interact and allowing the network to change the number of output channels.

Hence the two stages divide the job:

\[ \boxed{\text{Depthwise: spatial filtering}} \qquad \boxed{\text{Pointwise: channel mixing}} \] 
  depthwise (no mixing)           pointwise (all mixing, no spatial extent)

  ch1 ──[3×3]──► ch1              ch1 ┐
  ch2 ──[3×3]──► ch2              ch2 ├──[1×1×C_in]──► out_k
  ch3 ──[3×3]──► ch3              ch3 ┘

Together they reproduce what a standard convolution does in one step — but factorised, and far cheaper.

15. MobileNetV2 — What is an inverted residual block?

expansion → depthwise → projection
QuestionExplain the basic structure of a MobileNetV2 bottleneck block and why it is called an "inverted residual."

The MobileNetV2 block is

\[ \boxed{ \text{Expansion} \rightarrow \text{Depthwise} \rightarrow \text{Projection} } \]

together with a residual connection when the dimensions permit it. The input first passes through a \(1\times1\) expansion layer that increases the number of channels. A depthwise convolution then performs inexpensive spatial processing, and another \(1\times1\) projection compresses the representation back to a smaller number of channels.

          ┌──────────────── residual (when shapes match) ────────────────┐
          │                                                              ▼
  x ──────┴──► 1×1 expand ──► 3×3 depthwise ──► 1×1 project ──────────► (+)
            narrow → WIDE        WIDE            WIDE → narrow

It is called an inverted residual because traditional residual designs usually have relatively wide representations around a narrower internal bottleneck. MobileNetV2 reverses this: the input and output are relatively narrow, while the intermediate representation is expanded.

Expensive spatial convolution is still depthwise, so processing the expanded representation remains efficient — widening the middle costs little precisely because the middle stage is the cheap one.

16. MobileNetV1 vs MobileNetV2 — What changed?

V1 vs V2
QuestionWhat is the main architectural difference between MobileNetV1 and MobileNetV2?

MobileNetV1 mainly relies on repeated depthwise-separable convolution: a depthwise spatial convolution followed by a pointwise \(1\times1\) convolution. MobileNetV2 keeps that efficiency idea but introduces a bottleneck structure:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Expansion} \rightarrow \text{Depthwise} \rightarrow \text{Projection} } \]

together with a residual connection when possible.

The expansion layer first increases the channel dimension so features can be represented in a richer intermediate space. The depthwise layer performs cheap spatial processing, and the projection layer compresses the representation back down.

Therefore MobileNetV2 combines the computational efficiency of depthwise convolution with a residual-style bottleneck design.

17. EfficientNet — What is compound scaling?

★ highest-value conceptual question
QuestionWhat problem does EfficientNet address, and what is meant by compound scaling?

When increasing the capacity of a CNN there are three obvious choices: make it deeper, make it wider, or increase the input resolution. EfficientNet contrasts these individual strategies with compound scaling, where depth, width and resolution are increased together in a balanced manner rather than scaling only one dimension.

The intuition is that the three dimensions are related. Higher-resolution images provide more detailed information, but the network also needs enough depth to process increasingly complex features and enough width to represent them. Increasing only one dimension creates an imbalance — more pixels with no extra depth to interpret them, or more depth with no extra detail to work on.

EfficientNet therefore spends additional computation more systematically, scaling all three dimensions together.

Scale only…What you gainWhat becomes the bottleneck
Depthmore abstract featurestoo little detail and too few feature types to work with
Widthmore feature types per layertoo shallow to combine them into high-level structure
Resolutionfiner spatial detailreceptive field too small to cover the larger image
All three (compound)balanced capacity—

The resolution row connects back to the receptive field page: if you double the input resolution without adding depth, the final layer's receptive field covers half as much of the scene in relative terms. Resolution and depth genuinely have to move together.

18. Architecture Evolution — What fundamental problem was each architecture trying to solve?

the summary answer
QuestionBriefly explain the major design idea introduced by LeNet, AlexNet, ResNet, InceptionNet, MobileNet and EfficientNet.

The architectures can be understood as a progression of different CNN design problems. LeNet established the basic convolution–pooling hierarchy for learning spatial features. AlexNet scaled CNNs to complex natural images using a much deeper and wider architecture. ResNet addressed the difficulty of training very deep networks through identity shortcut connections. InceptionNet introduced parallel multi-scale processing and \(1\times1\) bottlenecks to obtain rich features while controlling computational cost.

MobileNet focused on deployment efficiency by replacing expensive standard convolution with depthwise-separable convolution, and MobileNetV2 further introduced expansion, depthwise processing and projection with inverted residual connections. EfficientNet approached efficiency from another direction: instead of changing the convolution itself, it systematically scales network depth, width and image resolution together using compound scaling.

The one-line map to memorise

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \text{LeNet}&:\ \text{basic CNN hierarchy}\\ \text{AlexNet}&:\ \text{scale CNNs up}\\ \text{ResNet}&:\ \text{train very deep CNNs}\\ \text{Inception}&:\ \text{multi-scale features efficiently}\\ \text{MobileNet}&:\ \text{cheap convolution}\\ \text{EfficientNet}&:\ \text{balanced model scaling} \end{aligned} } \]

And for the four most-examined ones

\[ \boxed{ \begin{array}{ll} \text{VGG} & \rightarrow\ \text{simple repeated }3\times3\text{ blocks}\\[2mm] \text{ResNet} & \rightarrow\ \text{shortcut / residual learning}\\[2mm] \text{Inception} & \rightarrow\ \text{parallel multi-scale branches}\\[2mm] \text{MobileNet} & \rightarrow\ \text{separate spatial filtering from channel mixing} \end{array} } \]

These are the core intuitions to be able to explain without memorising wording. If you can say what problem each architecture was solving, you can reconstruct the mechanism.
